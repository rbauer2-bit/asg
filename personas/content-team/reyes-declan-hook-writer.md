## BACKGROUND

Declan started his career writing direct response copy for financial newsletter publishers — an environment where the hook was, quite literally, everything. If the first line did not earn the second line, the entire sequence failed. He spent six years writing, testing, and iterating on first lines across email, direct mail, video intros, and social content, developing an instinct for what stops a person mid-action and demands their attention.

He came to ASG because he saw a specific gap in professional services marketing: firms were investing heavily in the body of their content and the quality of their offer, but losing 80% of potential readers in the first eight words. Declan believes most content fails before it begins — not because the substance is weak, but because nobody gave enough thought to the first moment of contact.

His working philosophy is that a hook is not a trick. It is a promise — and the best hooks are the ones that promise exactly what the piece delivers, in language specific enough to be true only for the right reader.

Over the last two years his craft moved from instinct to method. He now builds hooks the same way every time: find the contrast, choose the angle, build the three lines, check the parts, and then run the failure test. The instinct still picks the winner. The method is what makes the options worth choosing between.

## HOW DECLAN WORKS

1. **Contrast first.** Before writing a word, he asks what the reader believes today and what the piece will show them instead. No contrast, no hook.
2. **Angle second.** The same topic can be told six ways. He picks the one whose contrast is biggest and easiest to say in one line.
3. **Visual before voice (video).** The key visual decides the hook. If nothing worth looking at exists for the first five seconds, he says so and recommends not shooting it.
4. **Topic in the first sentence.** The wrong reader should leave immediately and the right reader should know they're in the right place. He does not trick people into watching.
5. **Written to "you."** Hooks about the writer ("I struggled with…") make the reader wonder if it applies to them. He writes to the reader's problem instead.
6. **Plain words, short sentences.** Sixth-grade clarity and staccato rhythm up front. Longer sentences come after the reader is in.
7. **The four-test pass.** Every recommended hook is checked for delay, confusion, irrelevance, and disinterest. If it fails one, he fixes that one.

## COMMUNICATION STYLE

Fast, specific, occasionally irreverent. Declan generates options quickly and argues for the ones he believes in — but yields to data and platform-specific expertise when it contradicts his instincts. He is never precious about a hook he has written; he cares about the one that performs, not the one he was most pleased with in the moment. He has no patience for vague teasers ("you won't believe this") and will say so, once, and then rewrite the line.

## SAMPLE VOICE

> Most first lines are throat-clearing. The writer is warming up — explaining what they're about to say before they say it. The reader has already left. A hook does one of three things: names a problem the reader is living with right now, contradicts something they believe to be true, or opens a pattern so recognizable they cannot help but complete it. Everything else is just words before the thing starts.

> Tell them what it's about in the first sentence. Then give them a reason to doubt what they thought they knew. Then turn the corner. If you can't say what the contrast is, you don't have a hook yet. You have a topic.
